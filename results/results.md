# 5. Key issues must be considered when designing asymmetric donors to achieve the commercial application of asy-OSCs

## Response A

Designing asymmetric donors for asymmetric organic solar cells (asy-OSCs) to achieve commercial application involves addressing several key issues to ensure high performance, stability, scalability, and cost-effectiveness. Below are the critical aspects that must be considered:

**1. Optimizing Energy Levels**
- **HOMO and LUMO Alignment**: The energy levels of the asymmetric donor must be carefully tuned to ensure efficient charge transfer to the acceptor. The HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) should be low enough to provide a high open-circuit voltage (Voc), while the LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) should align well with the acceptor's LUMO for efficient charge separation.
- **Energy Loss Minimization**: Minimize the energy loss to improve power conversion efficiency (PCE).

**2. Broad and Strong Light Absorption**
- **Asymmetric Molecular Design**: Asymmetric donors often exhibit broader and stronger absorption spectra due to their unique molecular structures. This enhances the light-harvesting capability of the solar cell. The donor should ideally absorb in complementary regions of the solar spectrum to the acceptor, ensuring maximal light utilization.
- **Near-Infrared (NIR) Absorption**: For tandem or high-efficiency single-junction devices, donors with extended absorption into the NIR region are desirable.

**3. Molecular Packing and Morphology**
- **Optimized Molecular Packing**: Asymmetric donors often exhibit unique packing behavior due to their structural asymmetry. This can influence the morphology of the active layer and charge transport properties. Proper molecular packing is essential for achieving high charge mobility and minimizing recombination losses.
- **Blend Morphology**: The donor-acceptor blend should form an ideal nanoscale phase separation with continuous charge transport pathways. Asymmetric donors may require specific processing conditions (e.g., solvent selection, annealing) to achieve optimal morphology.

**4. Charge Transport and Mobility**
- **Balanced Charge Transport**: The donor should exhibit high hole mobility to ensure balanced charge transport with the acceptor's electron mobility.
- **Reduced Recombination**: Asymmetric donors should facilitate efficient charge separation and transport, minimizing charge recombination losses.

**5. Stability**
- **Thermal Stability**: The donor material must withstand high temperatures during processing and operation without degrading or crystallizing.
- **Photochemical Stability**: The asymmetric donor should resist photochemical degradation under prolonged exposure to sunlight.
- **Morphological Stability**: The active layer morphology should remain stable over time to ensure long device lifetimes.

**6. Scalability and Processability**
- **Synthetic Simplicity**: The synthesis of asymmetric donors should involve fewer steps, low-cost starting materials, and high yields to reduce production costs.
- **Solution Processability**: The donor should be easily processable from solution to enable scalable manufacturing techniques like roll-to-roll printing or slot-die coating.
- **Solvent Compatibility**: Use of environmentally friendly solvents (e.g., water, alcohols) for processing is desirable to reduce toxicity and comply with commercial manufacturing standards.

**7. Compatibility with Non-Fullerene Acceptors (NFAs)**
- **Donor-Acceptor Interactions**: The asymmetric donor must exhibit strong compatibility with state-of-the-art NFAs to achieve high PCE. Proper energy level alignment, complementary absorption spectra, and favorable intermolecular interactions are critical.

# 10. Working Principles of NanoIR-AFM Technology and Instrumentation. The primary challenges facing the application of NanoIR-AFM that may limit its applicability in specific situations.

## Response A

**1. Working Principles of NanoIR-AFM Technology**

NanoIR-AFM combines atomic force microscopy (AFM) with infrared (IR) spectroscopy to provide nanoscale chemical, mechanical, and structural information. The key principles are:
- **AFM for Topography**: AFM uses a sharp tip to scan the sample surface, providing high-resolution topographical images.
- **IR Absorption**: A tunable IR laser irradiates the sample, causing localized thermal expansion where the sample absorbs IR light.
- **Photothermal Detection**: The AFM cantilever detects the thermal expansion as mechanical oscillations, correlating them with IR absorption at specific wavelengths.
- **Spectroscopic Analysis**: By sweeping the laser wavelength, NanoIR generates IR absorption spectra, revealing chemical composition at the nanoscale.

**2. Instrumentation of NanoIR-AFM**
- **AFM Unit**: Provides high-resolution imaging of surface morphology.
- **IR Laser Source**: A tunable or broadband IR laser illuminates the sample for spectroscopic analysis.
- **Cantilever and Tip**: Detects thermal expansion caused by IR absorption.
- **Detection System**: Monitors cantilever deflection or oscillation to measure IR absorption.
- **Control Software**: Integrates AFM imaging with IR spectroscopy for data acquisition and analysis.

This technology is particularly useful for studying polymers, biomaterials, and thin films at the nanoscale.

**3. The primary challenges facing the application of NanoIR-AFM (nanoscale infrared spectroscopy combined with atomic force microscopy) include:**
- **Spatial Resolution Limitations**: While NanoIR achieves nanoscale resolution, it may not resolve features below ~10 nm, limiting its use in highly complex or ultrafine structures.
- **Material Absorption Constraints**: NanoIR relies on IR absorption, so materials with weak IR absorption or overlapping absorption bands can yield poor spectral contrast.
